# How Did Humans Invent Money? (script for approval)

## Cold open
This is Byte. Byte just ordered the biggest burger in town.
He taps his card. Declined.
No cash. No coins. Nothing. Just a very hungry stickman.
The cook looks at his hoodie and says: I'll take that instead.
Wait. Can you really just trade stuff? For most of history, there were no coins, no cards, no apps.
So how did we go from swapping goats... to paying with numbers on a screen?
Let's go back. Way back.

## Chapter 1: The barter myth
Here's the story you probably learned at school. Before money, people bartered.
I have a goat. You have wheat. We swap.
But what if I don't want a goat?
Economists call this the double coincidence of wants. You need someone who has what you want, and wants what you have.
Here's the twist. Anthropologists have never found a society that ran purely on barter.
In small villages, people did something simpler. They kept track of favours.
You give me bread today. I owe you one. Everybody remembers.
In other words, debt came before coins.

## Chapter 2: Clay receipts
About five thousand years ago, in Mesopotamia, temples kept track of who owed what.
They wrote it down on clay tablets. Some of the oldest writing in the world isn't poetry. It's receipts.
Debts were counted in barley, and in silver, weighed in a unit called the shekel.
A shekel wasn't a coin. It was a weight. About eight grams of silver.

## Chapter 3: Shells and stones
Far away, people found other things to count with. Shells.
Cowrie shells were small, tough and hard to fake. They were used as money across Asia, Africa and the Indian Ocean for centuries.
In Chinese writing, the old symbol for a shell even became part of the words for money and buying.
And on the tiny Pacific island of Yap, money was enormous.
Giant stone discs called rai. The biggest are about three and a half metres wide, and weigh around four tonnes.
They were carved on another island, hundreds of kilometres away, and brought home by canoe and raft.
They were far too heavy to move. So nobody moved them. Everyone just remembered who owned which stone.
The story goes that one stone sank to the bottom of the sea on the way home. The family still counted it as theirs.
Money that nobody can see... but everybody agrees exists. Remember that. It comes back later.

## Chapter 4: The first coins
Around six hundred B.C., in a kingdom called Lydia, in what's now Turkey, someone had a brilliant idea.
Take lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, and stamp them with a lion's head.
The stamp meant: the king guarantees it. No more weighing.
These were some of the first coins in history.
A later king of Lydia, Croesus, made coins of pure gold and pure silver. People still say 'rich as Croesus'.
Coins spread to Greece, Persia and Rome. Soon, rulers were putting their own faces on them.

## Chapter 5: Paper
But coins have a problem. They're heavy.
In Sichuan, in China, people used iron coins worth so little that a big purchase needed a whole cart of them.
So merchants left their coins with trusted shops, and carried paper receipts instead.
In ten twenty-four, the Song government took over, and printed the world's first official paper money. The jiaozi.
When Marco Polo saw paper money in China, he could hardly believe it. Paper, worth as much as gold.
But printing money is tempting. Print too much, and prices explode. That's inflation.

## Chapter 6: Banks and gold
Europe caught up much later. In sixteen sixty-one, a bank in Sweden printed Europe's first banknotes.
For centuries, a banknote was a promise. Take it to the bank, and swap it for real gold or silver.
That's called the gold standard.
Then, in nineteen seventy-one, the United States ended the link between the dollar and gold.
Since then, most money is backed by one thing. Trust.

## Chapter 7: Invisible money
In nineteen fifty, a card called Diners Club let people eat now and pay later. It's often called the first modern credit card.
Then came cash machines, online banking, and tapping your phone.
And here's the wild part. In the UK, banknotes and coins are only about three percent of all money.
The rest is just numbers, sitting in bank computers.
Just like the stones of Yap. Money nobody can see... that everybody agrees exists.
In two thousand nine, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto launched Bitcoin. Money with no bank at all.
A year later, a programmer paid ten thousand bitcoins for two pizzas. Years later, those coins were worth hundreds of millions of dollars.

Which brings us back to Byte.
He opens his banking app. Turns out, he just forgot to move money onto his card.
Tap. Paid. In less than a second.
Five thousand years of clay, shells, stones, gold and paper...
all so Byte could buy a burger with a number on a screen.
