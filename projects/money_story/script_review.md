# How Did Humans Invent Money? (script v2 for approval)

## Cold open
Imagine you walk into a burger joint. You're starving.
You order the biggest burger on the menu. Double patty. Extra cheese.
You tap your card. Beep. Declined.
You check your pockets. No cash. No coins. Nothing.
The cook leans over the counter, looks at your hoodie, and says: I'll take that instead.
Would you do it? Would you trade the hoodie off your back for a burger?
Because for most of human history, that's not a joke. There were no coins. No cards. No apps.
So how did we go from trading hoodies and goats... to paying with invisible numbers on a screen?
And why does a piece of paper with a number on it buy you a burger at all?
Stick around, because the answer is weirder than you think.
It involves giant stones at the bottom of the ocean, a king so rich he became a saying, and pizzas that cost hundreds of millions of dollars.
Let's go back. Way back.

## Chapter 1: The barter myth
Here's the story you probably learned at school.
Before money, people bartered. You have a goat. I have wheat. We swap. Easy.
Except it isn't easy at all.
What if you have a goat, but I don't want a goat? I want shoes.
Now you have to find someone who has shoes, and also wants a goat.
Economists even have a name for this headache. The double coincidence of wants.
And here's the twist your textbook skipped.
When anthropologists went looking for a society that actually ran on barter like this, they couldn't find one.
Not one.
So what did people really do? Something much simpler.
Imagine you live in a small village. Your neighbour needs bread. You give her some.
She doesn't pay you. She owes you. And everybody in the village remembers.
Next month, when you need help fixing your roof, guess who shows up.
That's not barter. That's credit. In other words, debt came before coins.

## Chapter 2: Clay receipts
But memory only works in a small village. What happens when your village grows into a city?
About five thousand years ago, in Mesopotamia, in what's now Iraq, cities had exactly that problem.
So temple officials started writing debts down. On clay tablets.
And here's something wild. Some of the oldest writing on Earth isn't poetry, or prayers. It's receipts.
Debts were counted in barley, and in silver, measured in a unit called the shekel.
But a shekel wasn't a coin. It was a weight. About eight grams of silver.
There was just one problem with all this debt. It kept piling up.
Farmers who had a bad harvest fell behind. Some lost their land. Some even had to work off their debts as servants.
So every so often, a new king would do something amazing. He'd cancel the debts. All of them. A clean slate.
The Sumerians had a word for it. Amargi. It literally means 'return to mother', because people freed from debt could go home to their families.
Some historians call it one of the earliest written words we have for freedom.

## Chapter 3: Shells and stones
Meanwhile, in other parts of the world, people found other things to count with.
Like shells.
Cowrie shells were small, shiny, tough, and almost impossible to fake.
For centuries, they were used as money across Asia, Africa and the Indian Ocean.
In fact, in Chinese writing, the old symbol for a shell became part of the words for money, wealth and buying.
But if you think shells are strange, wait until you see the money on the island of Yap.
Yap is a tiny island in the Pacific Ocean. And its money was... huge.
Giant stone discs called rai. The biggest are about three and a half metres wide, and weigh around four tonnes.
That's heavier than a car.
The stones were carved on another island, hundreds of kilometres away, and brought home across the open ocean by canoe and raft.
Once they arrived, they were way too heavy to move. So nobody moved them.
When a stone changed owners, it stayed exactly where it was. Everyone just remembered who it belonged to.
And the story goes that one stone sank to the bottom of the sea on the way home.
Nobody ever saw it again. But the family that paid for it was still considered rich. Because everyone agreed it was theirs.
Think about that. Money that nobody can see, that everybody agrees exists.
Remember that idea. Because by the end of this video, you'll realise you use money exactly like that, every single day.

## Chapter 4: The first coins
Now let's talk about the money you can hold in your hand.
Around six hundred B.C., in a kingdom called Lydia, in what's now Turkey, someone had a brilliant idea.
Take lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, and stamp them with a lion's head.
That stamp was like a promise from the king. This lump is the real deal. No weighing needed.
Imagine how much faster shopping got. No scales. No arguing. Just hand over a coin.
These were some of the first coins in history.
A later king of Lydia, Croesus, went further. He made coins of pure gold and pure silver.
He became so famous for his wealth that, two and a half thousand years later, people still say 'rich as Croesus'.
Coins spread fast. To Greece. To Persia. To Rome.
And rulers loved them, because they could put their own faces on them. Free advertising, in every pocket.
But coins came with a temptation.
If you're an emperor and you need more money, why not just put less silver in each coin?
That's exactly what Rome did. Over a couple of hundred years, the silver in Roman coins fell from almost pure, to just a few percent.
People noticed. Prices soared. And trust in Roman money crumbled.
Remember that word. Trust. It's the secret ingredient of all money.

## Chapter 5: Paper
There's another problem with coins. They're heavy.
In Sichuan, in China, people used iron coins worth so little that a big purchase meant hauling a whole cart of them.
So merchants came up with a clever trick. Leave your heavy coins with a trusted shop, and carry a paper receipt instead.
In ten twenty-four, the Song government took over, and printed the world's first official paper money. The jiaozi.
When the traveller Marco Polo saw paper money in China a couple of centuries later, he could hardly believe it. Paper, worth as much as gold.
But printing money is even more tempting than shaving coins.
Need money? Just print more!
Print too much, and every note buys less and less. That's called inflation, and China learned that lesson the hard way, more than once.

## Chapter 6: Banks and gold
Europe was much slower. And Sweden tried something... interesting.
In the sixteen hundreds, Sweden had lots of copper. So it made copper money. Giant copper money.
The biggest was a copper plate weighing almost twenty kilograms. Imagine paying for your groceries with that.
No surprise, people preferred paper. In sixteen sixty-one, a Swedish bank printed Europe's first banknotes.
For centuries after that, a banknote was a promise. Take it to the bank, and you could swap it for real gold or silver.
That's called the gold standard.
Then, in nineteen seventy-one, the United States ended the link between the dollar and gold.
Since then, the money in your pocket isn't backed by gold. It's backed by one thing. Trust.
And when trust breaks, things get crazy.
In Germany in nineteen twenty-three, prices rose so fast that workers were paid twice a day, and rushed to spend their wages before they lost value.
In two thousand nine, Zimbabwe printed a one hundred trillion dollar note. Within weeks, it was almost worthless.
And when people have no official money at all, they simply invent their own.
In some American prisons, where cash isn't allowed, inmates have used packets of mackerel as money.
Fish. Shells. Stones. Paper. Money is anything enough people agree it is.

## Chapter 7: Invisible money
Now let's fast forward to you.
In nineteen fifty, a card called Diners Club let people eat now and pay later. It's often called the first modern credit card.
Then came cash machines, online banking, and tapping your phone.
So here's a question. How much of all the money out there do you think is actual cash? Half? A quarter?
In the UK, notes and coins are only about three percent of all money.
The rest? Just numbers, sitting in bank computers.
Remember the stone at the bottom of the sea? Money nobody can see, that everybody agrees exists?
That's your bank balance.
And some people wondered: what if money didn't need banks at all?
In two thousand nine, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto launched Bitcoin. Digital money, with no bank in charge.
A year later, a programmer in Florida paid ten thousand bitcoins for two pizzas.
Years later, those coins were worth hundreds of millions of dollars. Probably the most expensive pizzas in history.

So, back to that burger joint.

No goat. No shells. No four-tonne stone. Just a number moving from one computer to another.
Five thousand years of clay, shells, stones, gold and paper... all so you could buy a burger with a number on a screen.
And it works for one simple reason. Everyone agrees it does.
